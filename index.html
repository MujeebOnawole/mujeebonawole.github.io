Journal of Cheminformatics, 2026

How do you evaluate explainability?

This foundation study sets out four tests. A key challenge is activity cliffs, where very similar molecules differ sharply in activity. We compared three approaches: a random forest using chemical features and functional groups, a CNN reading one-line molecular text (SMILES), and a relational graph network (RGCN) reading molecular graphs.

On activity cliffs, RGCN agreed slightly better with experimental evidence than CNN. In one case, its explanation made better sense for an inactive molecule even though the model wrongly predicted it as active.

Which parts of a molecule fight which microbe?

Graph models for S. aureus, E. coli and C. albicans show which fragments raise or lower predicted activity against each one, giving chemists a guide for design.

Learned from more than 120,000 compounds. In a separate screen of about 100,000, its selections found active compounds up to nearly 20 times more often than expected by chance.

ChemRxiv preprint, 2026

Which carbon capture predictions are worth acting on?

PoreGCN shows a model both the atoms and the pores of metal-organic frameworks, then labels how far each prediction can be trusted.

For separating carbon dioxide from nitrogen, more than 8 in 10 trustworthy test predictions were within 20 percent of the reference value. Fewer than 1 in 10 untrustworthy ones were.

Electronic properties of metal complexes from SMILES

One model predicts seven electronic properties of transition-metal complexes, shows which atoms matter and gives a trust label. The app needs no programming or quantum chemistry expertise.

Learned from about 100,000 metal complexes. On test examples, it explained about 95 percent of the variation in two important energy levels.

Molecular Informatics, 2026

Which metal will a molecule grab, and how strongly?

Enter a molecule as a line of text (called SMILES) and choose a metal. CoordBind-XAI predicts which atoms bind, how strong the binding is, which atoms drive the answer and how reliable it is. It needs no 3D structure.

Learned from about 20,000 measured binding strengths across more than 60 metals. In tests on new types of molecule, it explained nearly 90 percent of the variation in binding strength.

bioRxiv preprint, 2026

Where does a protein model stop being reliable?

Labels predictions of protein melting temperature and tracks how far each protein is from the training data. Plastic-degrading enzymes provide a case study, where absolute predictions break down but ranking survives.

On protein families not seen in training, predicted melting temperatures were off by about 4°C on average. For the most trusted predictions, this fell to about 3°C.

bioRxiv preprint, 2026

Can this person’s genetic risk score be trusted?

An overall accuracy figure cannot say whether one person’s genetic risk score is reliable. TrustPGS gives each person a trust label and tests how well the labels carry across ancestries.

After a simple adjustment, the trust labels remained useful in non-European populations for 5 of 10 traits, but not for all.

bioRxiv preprint, 2026

When can a DNA sequence model be relied on?

Predicts how accessible a stretch of DNA is, then checks model agreement, whether known DNA motifs support the explanation, and how unfamiliar the sequence is.

When the models agreed in tests, predictions supported by known DNA patterns had roughly 10 percent less error.

When should we trust an AI prediction?

AI models can now predict whether a molecule will kill bacteria, how well a material will capture carbon dioxide, or a person’s genetic risk of disease. Even a good model is sometimes wrong, and its overall accuracy does not tell you which predictions those are. That matters when each prediction decides the next step, such as which compound a chemist makes, which material an engineer tests, or how a doctor reads a risk score.

My work gives every prediction its own trust label. Several models make the same prediction and each one explains it by showing which parts of the molecule, material or DNA sequence drove the answer. When the models agree on both the answer and the reason, the prediction is labelled trustworthy. When they disagree, it is flagged for a closer look before anyone acts on it. In a study of carbon-capture materials, more than 8 in 10 predictions labelled trustworthy were close to the reference value, compared with fewer than 1 in 10 of the flagged ones.

Explainability is what makes this possible. It lets a scientist see why the model reached its answer, judge whether that reason makes sense, and decide with confidence whether to move forward. The aim is AI that is safer and more reliable to use for real decisions, in any field where a wrong prediction has a cost.
